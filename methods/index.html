IBF Routine v1: Method and Design Note

1. Purpose

This note documents the first complete version of the impact-based forecasting (IBF) product for the project pilots in Guatemala and Barbados. The routine converts the modelling team's event outputs (flood depth, velocity and exceedance-probability grids sampled onto buildings, roads and administrative units) into operational warning maps in which every receptor carries a warning colour.

2. Review of the preliminary analysis

The team's event-1 package contains, for Guatemala, a deterministic depth grid, four ensemble exceedance grids (depth of 7.62, 10, 30 and 76 cm or more), a land-use grid, and a GeoPackage of 4,686 buildings, 458 road segments and 2 municipal units with raster values sampled per feature. For Barbados it contains depth, velocity and hazard-class grids and a GeoPackage of 178,084 buildings, 55,846 road segments and 609 enumeration districts with census population. The main gaps that kept this from being a final product:

  1. Likelihood and impact were never combined into a warning level anywhere. Guatemala has likelihood but no impact classification; Barbados has a hazard class but no likelihood.
  2. No map product existed, only data tables and grids.
  3. Legends were inconsistent and non-standard across the two pilots.
  4. Unit ambiguities: Barbados depth stored as integers (assumed cm) and velocity in a grid whose values imply mm/s despite its label.
  5. Guatemala population spreads full municipal totals over pilot-area buildings only, overstating occupancy (about 62 persons per building).
  6. Internal inconsistencies: 6,469 Barbados buildings with sampled depth of 0.3 m or more carry hazard class 0, and the warning-flag fields in Guatemala are all zero.

3. The adopted standard

The product follows Speight et al. (2018), the Glasgow operational surface-water IBF pilot, whose framework is the Scottish Flood Forecasting Service and UK Flood Guidance Statement standard. The warning level of every feature is the matrix combination of likelihood and potential impact severity, rendered green, yellow, amber and red.

Likelihood band (FGS)Probability of occurrence
Very Lowbelow 20 percent
Low20 to 40 percent
Medium40 to 60 percent
Highabove 60 percent

Probabilities below 5 percent fall under the reporting threshold. Likelihood comes from the ensemble flood-map library: the probability of exceeding 10 cm feeds the Minor threshold, 30 cm feeds Significant, and 76 cm feeds Severe. A feature's colour is the worst matrix cell across those severity-specific probabilities, which is the paper's approach evaluated per receptor instead of per kilometre cell.

ReceptorMinimalMinorSignificantSevere
Buildings (people and property)below 0.1 m0.1 to 0.3 m0.3 to 0.76 m0.76 m and deeper
Roads (transport)below 0.1 m0.1 to 0.3 m0.3 to 0.5 m0.5 m and deeper

Where velocity exists (Barbados), severity is upgraded with the flood hazard rating HR = d x (v + 0.5) + 0.5 (urban debris factor): an HR of 1.25 or more gives at least Significant and 2.0 or more gives Severe, the danger-to-people classes of FD2320 and FD2321. District overall risk aggregates feature results through thresholds adapted from the paper's Table 1: at least one flooded residential property gives Minor; any residential property at Significant severity or worse, more than 2 non-residential receptors flooded, or more than 5 m of road at Significant severity gives Significant; more than 100 residential properties at Significant severity or worse gives Severe. The class is then combined with district likelihood through the matrix. All thresholds are configuration values, not code.

4. Event-1 results

Guatemala: 164 buildings yellow and 80 amber along the Rio Villalobos corridor, 8.2 km of road at yellow or worse, and both municipios at LOW overall risk, a correctly quiet product for an event seen by one ensemble member in five. Barbados: 23,371 buildings yellow, 8,487 amber and 5,469 red, 774 km of road at amber or worse, and 365 districts at MEDIUM with 33 at HIGH under the deterministic-likelihood assumption.

5. Assumptions to confirm and data requested

  1. Ensemble exceedance grids for Barbados (the sample numbering implies more members exist). This replaces the deterministic-likelihood assumption, the main gap in v1.
  2. A larger ensemble for Guatemala: five members quantize probability to 20 percent steps.
  3. Corrected population disaggregation for Guatemala (census blocks or WorldPop), so population exposed becomes reportable.
  4. Critical-facility receptors (health, schools, emergency services, utilities) to activate the community-services thresholds, which v1 proxies with non-residential buildings.
  5. Confirmation of Barbados units (depth in cm, velocity in mm/s) and of the meaning of the residential classes.

6. Iteration plan

v1.1 applies the team's answers on units and ensembles and tunes district thresholds jointly. v1.2 adds the time dimension (per-forecast-cycle maps and a worst-cell-in-24-hours header, as in the FEWS Glasgow reports) and critical facilities as a third receptor group. v2 connects the routine to the live likelihood outputs of the operational pipeline so each forecast cycle publishes these maps automatically.
